Appendix A

Grounding & Bonding Verification Procedure

Supports Section E. Use this procedure to actually verify each grounding item rather than eyeballing it — a station can look grounded and still have a broken bond, a corroded lug, or a lost RF ground that only shows up as a ruined SWR match or a shock hazard. Work through 1–5 in order; each depends on the one before it.

  • Digital multimeter (ohms/continuity)
  • Ground resistance tester (clamp-on or fall-of-potential), if available
  • Wire brush / fine sandpaper
  • Nut driver / wrench set
  • Insulated gloves

A.1 — concept Single-Point Ground System

  1. Confirm the station has one common ground reference — a bus bar or heavy copper strap that every piece of equipment bonds to — rather than each item running to its own separate rod or cold-water pipe. Separate grounds at different earth potentials is what turns a lightning strike into a ground loop that arcs across the room.
  2. Trace where AC safety ground (green wire), RF/station ground, and lightning ground meet. Per NEC practice they should bond together at one point near where service/antenna grounds enter the structure.

A.2 — antenna system Mast/Tower and Feedline Ground

  1. Visually inspect the ground rod(s) at the antenna base for corrosion, a loose clamp, or exposed/nicked bonding wire.
  2. Confirm the rod is bonded to the station ground with a short, direct run of at least #10 AWG copper wire or 3/4" copper strap — flat strap is preferred over round wire because it carries RF and transient surge current with less inductance.
  3. Continuity test: meter between the antenna mount/mast and the ground rod clamp. Expect well under 1 Ω. A high or unstable reading means a corroded or loose bond — clean the contact point and retest.
  4. If a ground resistance tester is available, measure the rod-to-earth resistance. Target ≤ 25 Ω (NEC minimum for a single rod), ideally ≤ 10 Ω for lightning protection. If it's high, bond in an additional rod at least 6 ft away.
  5. Confirm a coax lightning arrestor/surge suppressor is inline on the feedline and its ground lug bonds to the same ground point — not a separate one.
  6. Confirm there is a way to disconnect and ground the feedline when the station is not in use, particularly for portable/mobile setups during storm season.
Common finding: a arrestor installed but its ground lead run to a nearby fence post or downspout instead of the station ground — this defeats the single-point ground and should be corrected on the spot.

A.3 — transceiver Chassis Ground

  1. Locate the chassis ground lug on the rear panel (separate from the DC power or coax connections).
  2. Confirm a dedicated ground strap runs from that lug directly to the station ground bus — the AC power cord's safety ground is not a substitute for this bond.
  3. Keep the strap as short as physically possible, and well under a quarter-wavelength of the lowest operating frequency, so it can't become a resonant radiator and re-introduce RF into the shack. Flat braid is preferred over round wire for the same low-inductance reason as A.2.
  4. Check the mounting point is bare metal — no paint or anodizing under the lug — and the hardware (including a star washer) is tight.
  5. Continuity test chassis-to-ground-bus: expect well under 1 Ω.

A.4 — tuner Antenna Tuner Ground

  1. Locate the tuner's dedicated ground terminal — separate from the coax shield connection.
  2. Confirm it bonds directly to the station ground bus with a short, heavy strap. Many external tuners rely on this connection as part of the RF counterpoise, especially when matching a random-wire or end-fed antenna — it is not just a safety ground.
  3. If the operator reports the tuner can no longer find a match on a band it used to tune cleanly, check this bond first before suspecting the antenna itself — a lost or corroded tuner ground is the most common cause.
  4. For end-fed or random-wire antennas, confirm the counterpoise wire(s) specified by the tuner's manual are present, undamaged, and connected — the tuner ground alone is rarely sufficient without them.

A.5 — associated equipment Power Supplies, Amplifiers, Switches & Interfaces

  1. Bond the metal chassis of every associated item — power supply, amplifier, coax switch, digital-mode/logging interface — to the same ground bus used in A.3. Never give one item its own independent ground rod; two ground points at different potentials is how ground loops and hum/RFI get introduced.
  2. For a coax switch, verify the body is grounded and check continuity through each position, not just the one currently in use.
  3. For a computer interface used for digital modes or NetLog-style logging, note whether it is opto-isolated. A non-isolated interface is a common source of RF feedback and ground-loop hum and may need an isolation transformer or ferrite choking added.

A.6 — safety Precautions While Testing

  1. Never test or work on the grounding system during a thunderstorm or when lightning is possible in the area.
  2. De-energize the station before disconnecting or disturbing any bonding strap.
  3. Use insulated gloves and insulated-handle tools when probing near an energized station.
  4. After any known lightning event near the station, re-inspect and re-test the entire ground system in full before returning the station to service.
